[Tansa](https://tansa.app) is an album recommendation site: give it an album you like, or a vibe you are after, and it finds the closest albums by how they sound and the mood they evoke, laid out on a galaxy-like map. This post applies reinforcement learning (RL) to Tansa in three stages: a bandit that learns from likes and skips in the visitor's browser, a GRPO implementation written from scratch and checked against a reference library, and a study that trains a 1.5B-parameter language model to rank albums from a listener's history. When a small language model is rewarded for guessing a listener's next albums, does it learn taste, or does it learn to recommend what is popular?

## Three stages, and a protocol written first

Each stage had its evaluation written before any result existed: the metrics, the pass criteria, the hypotheses, and the rule that tuning happens on validation data while the test users are touched once, by a single script at the end. A disappointing result is reported as it is; a broken pipeline is fixed, re-run and logged. Every call made along the way went into a numbered list of assumptions with the condition that would reverse it, so each stage below states its criterion first and then what was measured.

## Stage 1: a bandit in the browser

The live feature is a "Tune to me" button on Tansa's recommendation list. The ten rows with previews gain a yes and a no, an answer removes its row and the rest reorder at once, and everything is forgotten when the visitor leaves.

Underneath is a linear Thompson sampling bandit. A bandit treats each album as an arm, keeps a model of the reward each arm would earn, and picks what to show next while learning from the answers. Here the model is a Bayesian linear regression from four features of an album to its reward: its 64-dimensional audio vector minus the seed album's (the vectors the site ranks by), the squared distance between the two, the weight of the album's row on the page's own list, and an intercept. Rewards are +1 for a yes, −0.25 for a no and +0.25 for a clip heard for 20 seconds or to its end. Thompson sampling draws a weight vector from the posterior and ranks the arms by it, so an album the model is unsure about sometimes comes to the top, which is the exploration. The candidates are the seed's 200 nearest albums in sound plus the page's own rows, fixed for the session, so the ranking never leaves the seed's neighbourhood. A ranking is 64 posterior draws and takes 2.7 ms in the browser, and the share of draws that put an album in the top ten is logged beside every row shown, as its propensity, so the logs can later evaluate other policies.

The intercept came from a bug. Without it, every prior score sat between −1.2 and −0.4 while every reward was −0.25 or above, so the first skip read as good news and moved the posterior toward the skipped album. The intercept takes the level of the rewards, and only differences between albums move the taste weights.

Tansa has too few visitors to test on, so the offline check uses a simulated listener built from [ListenBrainz](https://listenbrainz.org), the open listening-history dataset stage 3 trains on. For each of 2,000 held-out listeners the seed is the last album of their training history, the simulator scores an album by its sound distance to that history, and a session is twelve answers. Four policies ran on identical candidates and simulator seeds: `fixed`, today's page order, never updated; `random`; `greedy`, the mean of liked vectors minus the mean of skipped ones, re-ranked after every answer; and `bandit`, the shipped code ported operation for operation. The metric that matters is held-out recall: of the albums the listener went on to play that were among the candidates, the share in the final ten they walk away with.

Two criteria were preregistered. The bandit had to sit within 0.01 of greedy or above it, since it pays for exploration, and it does: 0.022 above, paired interval [0.014, 0.030]. It also had to beat the fixed page, and it does not: 0.012 below, interval [−0.021, −0.002]. The simulator scores taste with the same audio vectors the page ranks by, and the seed sits inside the history it scores against, so the page's nearest-first list is already close to the simulated listener's optimum, and twelve answers of exploration cost more than they earn.

The third criterion was an off-policy check: can the bandit's logs, each row with its propensity, estimate how a different policy would have done? SNIPS (self-normalised inverse propensity scoring, which reweights logged rewards by one over the propensity) and MIPS (the same over 16 sound clusters of the albums) had to land inside the interval of greedy's directly measured hit rate, 0.271 [0.268, 0.275]. They gave 0.278 and 0.292, both above, so this check is not met either: 64 tempered draws put most of the top-ten mass on a few dozen of the roughly 150 candidates, so greedy's list is covered unevenly, and the covered part is the part the bandit also likes.

Two exploratory runs on 1,000 validation listeners, labelled as such and never a criterion, say where this leads. Over longer visits the bandit's deficit against the page shrinks, from −0.019 at twelve answers to −0.003 at 24, and turns to +0.013 at 48. And an exploration floor fixes the logs: when with probability 0.1 the ten rows shown are uniform draws from the unanswered candidates, the SNIPS estimate moves from 0.289 to 0.269 and lands on the direct value, while MIPS at 0.288 still sits above. The floor costs 0.06 likes per twelve-answer session, 3.49 against 3.54, about one like in seventeen sessions, and raises held-out recall by 0.010.

The bandit learns 67 weights. The next two stages move to a language model and the policy-gradient methods used to post-train one.

## Stage 2: GRPO from scratch, matched to TRL

A policy gradient method trains a model that writes an answer token by token, each token a choice with a probability. REINFORCE multiplies the log-probability of every token the model chose by how good the whole answer turned out, takes the gradient, and so shifts probability toward the choices that led to good answers. Everything after that is variance reduction: "how good" needs a baseline, or every answer looks good and the model sharpens whatever it already does. GRPO ([Shao et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)) samples a group of answers to the same prompt, here eight, and uses their mean reward as the baseline, so "good" means "better than the other seven attempts at this prompt". A clipped probability ratio from PPO keeps a second update on the same batch from moving any token too far, and a KL penalty against a frozen copy of the starting model keeps the policy near it.

Before writing a line, we read the reference we would match, `trl.GRPOTrainer` at version 1.17.0, end to end. Its defaults differ from the GRPO paper in four places, among them a loss that weighs every token in the batch the same rather than averaging per answer, and each became a named switch in the from-scratch loss with the paper's value one flag away. One thing to know before reading any GRPO run: on-policy the probability ratio is exactly 1, so the loss value is minus the mean advantage and carries no signal. The reward, the KL and the clip fractions are what to read.

The match was preregistered in three parts. First, loss and gradients: on Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct with a LoRA adapter, the same prompts, completions, rewards and reference log-probabilities go through TRL's loss and ours at nine option sets on three random batches, 27 cases, and the relative error on the 96 adapter gradients had to stay under 1e-4 in float32 and 1e-2 in bfloat16. Float32 is met at 1.8e-5, and in float64 the two agree to 1.2e-6; the residual is reduction order. Bfloat16 is not met, 4.8e-2 against 1e-2, and measuring each implementation against its own float32 result says why: each one's bfloat16 gradient sits 34% from its own float32 gradient, so the criterion asked two bfloat16 computations to agree about thirty times more closely than either agrees with itself. The 1e-2 was a naive number. It stays in the protocol, reported as not met beside the float64 evidence.

Second, training behaviour: three seeds of 200 steps on the same prompt stream, with the mean reward over the last 20% of steps agreeing within one pooled seed standard deviation.

The from-scratch GRPO curve in blue and the TRL GRPOTrainer curve in orange rise together from about 0.05 to about 0.22 and overlap for the whole run; a REINFORCE curve in grey rises to about 0.12.">

The two curves lie on top of each other and their ten-step rolling means correlate at 0.987. Note what they do not show: the task was a ranking rule stated in the prompt, and neither implementation learned it. Both learned the answer format within 30 steps and then sat near random.

That was the third part, the learning check: REINFORCE and GRPO each had to reach twice the random policy's score on a toy ranking task. At 0.5B they did not, even on a task built to be learnable by construction, where every candidate line carries a numeric cue and sorting by the cue scores 0.93: 0.209 and 0.205 against a bar of 0.415. Sampling the base model explained it: the 0.5B model's greedy answer is the first candidates in the order shown, whatever the cues say. GRPO can only amplify what the base policy already does sometimes, and when no sample is right for the right reason the group-relative advantage rewards lucky ids and the reward stays flat. At 1.5B, the study's model, the check is met: the zero-shot answer already scores 0.367, partly sorting by the cue, and GRPO climbs to 0.844 and REINFORCE to 0.796 in 120 steps, three seeds each. The lesson carried into stage 3: measure the zero-shot start before deciding what a flat reward curve means.

## Stage 3: does GRPO teach taste or popularity?

The training data is ListenBrainz's public listening dump, 2.92 billion listens streamed through a filter, of which 369.8 million land on one of Tansa's 10,467 albums once joined to the catalog through MusicBrainz release groups. That leaves 47,890 listeners with twenty or more catalog albums, split by listener into 38,228 for training, 4,781 for validation and 4,881 for test, with each listener's last 20% of albums held out as what they went on to play.

The model sees a listener's twenty most recent albums, as artist and title, and 50 candidates in the genre of the most recent one: one to five of the listener's held-out albums and in-genre albums they never played, shuffled with a fixed seed. It answers with ten candidate ids in order. The reward is NDCG@10, which scores a list by where the held-out albums land in it (first place earns more than tenth), minus 0.1 if the answer is anything other than exactly ten distinct candidate ids. Negatives are drawn two ways, uniform over the genre or matched to the positives' popularity decile, the harder one, which gives two test columns. The 3,238 test prompts per sampler were written once and fingerprinted.

Four baselines need no language model: `random`; `popularity`, by listener count; `audio_knn`, by distance to the mean of the history in the audio vectors, which is what Tansa does today in spirit; and `item_cf`, collaborative filtering (EASE) fit on the training listeners, the standard recommender that learns which albums the same people play. The language-model methods all use Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct with the same LoRA shape and prompt: `zero_shot`; `sft`, trained on the true held-out order for 300 steps of eight prompts; `dpo`, from pairs of rankings with the better one by NDCG preferred; `grpo`, the from-scratch GRPO of stage 2 for 100 steps of four prompts by eight completions, rewarded with NDCG@10; and `grpo_debiased`, the same reward with each held-out album's gain weighted by one over its popularity to a power alpha, picked on validation and frozen before the test run.

Besides accuracy, every method is scored on what it does to the catalog: `coverage@10`, the share of offered albums it ever recommends; `long_tail_share`, the share of its slots filled by albums in the bottom 80% by listeners; `popularity_lift`, the mean log listener count of what it recommends minus that of what the listener went on to play; the Gini of its recommendation counts; and `map_drift`, where its recommendations land on Tansa's map. Four hypotheses were written down: `grpo` scores above `sft` and `zero_shot` (H1); it covers less of the catalog and lifts popularity more than `sft` and `item_cf` (H2); the debiased reward recovers at least half of that lost coverage while keeping 80% of the accuracy gain (H3); and a prompt with the album names replaced by opaque ids, a year, a genre and a sound similarity costs `zero_shot` more accuracy than `grpo`, which would mean the base model leans on what it remembers about the albums (H4).

The baselines ran first, on validation, and they set the bar; on the test prompts with uniform negatives they score `item_cf` 0.613, `popularity` 0.524, `audio_knn` 0.194 and `random` 0.127, and the language-model rows, three seeds each for the trained ones, sit at the bottom: `sft` 0.140, `sft_grpo` 0.139 and `sft_grpo_debiased` 0.139 a little above `random`, `dpo` 0.126 level with it and `zero_shot` 0.107 below.

Then the language model, on a 500-listener validation subset, NDCG@10 under uniform then popularity-matched negatives. `zero_shot` scores 0.109 and 0.098, below `random` at 0.125 and 0.127, for the same reason as at 0.5B: 52% of its greedy picks are among the first ten candidates shown. A 100-step GRPO pilot from that base model learned the format (well-formed answers from 66% to 92%) and nothing else: NDCG on its training prompts by quarter was 0.136, 0.126 and 0.145 against 0.124 for random. The assumption written for this case then applied: the study's GRPO family starts from the `sft` adapter, with that adapter as its KL reference. GRPO from the base model was planned as a three-seed control; that control was skipped on cost after the pilot's flat curve, and the evaluation lists it as missing by name.

`sft` itself scores 0.144 ± 0.011 over three seeds and 0.126 ± 0.002: under uniform negatives a sliver above random, under popularity-matched negatives nothing measurable, with coverage (0.47) and long-tail share (0.72) the same as random's, so it spreads its picks like a random policy. GRPO from that start does not move it: `sft_grpo` scores 0.137 ± 0.017 and 0.128 ± 0.005 at the same coverage, after 100 steps. And the sweep of the debiasing weight is flat.

The test run settles the question. On the 3,238 test prompts per sampler, three seeds for every trained method, the fine-tuned models sit within a hundredth of each other and of random: under uniform negatives `sft` 0.140, `sft_grpo` 0.139, `sft_grpo_debiased` 0.139, `random` 0.127, `dpo` 0.126 and `zero_shot` 0.107, and under popularity-matched negatives 0.130, 0.130, 0.131, 0.125, 0.122 and 0.099, against `item_cf` at 0.613 and 0.428. A hypothesis counts as supported only when every paired interval it needs lies wholly on its side of zero, a rule fixed while the passes were still running, and under it all four are unsupported on both samplers. H1: GRPO from the SFT adapter scores −0.0012 [−0.0045, +0.0022] against `sft` under uniform negatives and +0.0009 [−0.0020, +0.0036] under popularity-matched ones, the one difference whose mean has the hypothesised sign, and +0.032 [+0.026, +0.039] against `zero_shot`, a gain the SFT start accounts for. H2: its coverage is +0.0004 [−0.0020, +0.0028] against `sft` and 0.33 above `item_cf`, and its popularity lift −0.0045 [−0.0101, +0.0011] against `sft` and 2.08 below `item_cf`, so there is no popularity collapse to measure: the policy recommends albums about as unpopular as a random pick, and far less popular than the collaborative filter's. H3 is not applicable, since GRPO lost no coverage for the debiased reward to recover. H4: replacing the album names with opaque ids, a year, a genre and a sound similarity costs `zero_shot` 0.069 and `sft_grpo` 0.073 of NDCG@10 under uniform negatives (0.064 and 0.066 under popularity-matched), a difference of −0.0039 [−0.0109, +0.0032], so the fine-tuned model leans on the names as much as the base model does, and neither makes anything of the sound-similarity number, which `audio_knn` alone turns into 0.194.

The opaque prompts also showed what fine-tuning on one answer format does to another. The fine-tuned seeds differ in whether they answer an opaque prompt in its `c<j>` codes at all: seed 2 of the SFT-based methods answers in codes on 89 to 99% of prompts, seed 1 mostly with years or bare indices, and `dpo` with bare indices on every seed, while the same seeds' named outputs are well formed. The strict format rule stays the one scored, and no lenient reading was added after the fact.

One question the flat rows raise is whether 100 steps was too few. An exploratory run, on validation only and never a criterion, continued GRPO from the SFT adapter to 500 steps with a snapshot every 100.

NDCG@10, averaged over both samplers, reads 0.143, 0.130, 0.134, 0.138 and 0.140 at the five snapshots, each within a half-width of 0.016 to 0.017 of `sft` at 0.135 and `sft_grpo` at 0.133, and coverage stays between 0.482 and 0.484 against 0.481. Training five times longer would not have helped, at this reward and this batch.

## Keeping the numbers honest

Before the first test-set run, a separate reviewer read the study code with the data in hand, hunting for leakage, metric bugs, unfair baselines and ways to misfile an output. The substance held, with every stored baseline metric reproduced to 0.0 by the reviewer's own code; what it found was process: the debiasing weight was still a placeholder in the config; the evaluation script did not know the names of the SFT-initialised GRPO family; the pilot adapter, trained on uniform negatives only, must not pose as a control seed; the copies of the test files on the GPU host had never been checked against the fingerprints; and the opaque prompt files printed "nan" as the genre on 1,632 history lines, because a missing label is a floating-point NaN and NaN is truthy. Everything was fixed and logged before a test prompt was read.

An independent replication then re-implemented every stage 3 metric from the protocol text alone, without reading the study's metric code, and ran it on the same saved outputs: on validation, NDCG@10, hit rate, recall, format rate, coverage, Gini and popularity lift agree to 0.00000 across 359 compared rows, and on the test set none of 312 comparisons across the nine methods was flagged, with its parse of every completion agreeing with the stored one. It also found two places where the protocol admitted two readings (the averaging unit of `popularity_lift` and of `long_tail_share`), fixed in the changelog before any test number existed. The evaluation gate checks the 20 fingerprints first and exits non-zero naming every criterion that is missing or failed; its final run reads 58 passed, 4 failed and 2 missing. The four failures all sit outside stage 3, the bandit against the page, the two off-policy estimators and bfloat16, each reported above; the two missing rows are the base-model `grpo` and `grpo_debiased` controls, skipped on cost after the pilot's flat curve. Training spend over every run manifest came to $24.32 at list price and the study's training and sampling together to about $40, against the $30 of GPU time the project was budgeted; the GPU account's own meter read $59.48 at the end, $29.48 of it charged after the monthly $30 credit.

## Conclusions

So what did we learn? The bandit works as shipped and learns in the browser, but under a simulated listener built from the same audio vectors the page ranks by, twelve answers of exploration earn less than the page's own list, 0.110 against 0.122 on held-out recall; the deficit closes over a visit of two dozen answers and turns positive at four dozen. Its logs are the right shape for off-policy evaluation and explore too little to be used for it, which a 0.1 exploration floor fixes at the price of about one like in seventeen sessions.

Our GRPO matches TRL to 1.8e-5 in float32 across nine option sets, and the one criterion it misses, 1e-2 in bfloat16, was a number bfloat16 arithmetic cannot meet with itself. The learning check taught the most: GRPO amplifies what the base policy already does sometimes, so a 0.5B model that answers with the first candidates shown gives it nothing to amplify, while a 1.5B model that partly sorts by the cue passes the bar within 40 steps.

On the study's question, the test set says that at about $40 of GPU time the language-model recommenders sit a long way below a collaborative filter fit on the same listeners: `sft` at 0.140 against `item_cf` at 0.613 under uniform negatives, and under popularity-matched negatives 0.130 against 0.428, with random at 0.125. GRPO from the SFT start moves nothing, within 0.001 of `sft` on accuracy and 0.0004 on coverage, the debiased reward has nothing to recover, and five times the steps gives the same flat line. So the question this post opened with has a narrower answer than we expected: at this scale GRPO learned neither taste nor popularity, because its starting policy had nothing for the reward to amplify, which is the stage 2 lesson again on real data. What the opaque prompts add is that the fine-tuned model still leans on the album names as much as the base model does. Either answer was going to be a result, and the baselines were run first so that an LLM table without `item_cf` beside it could never read as progress.

## Limitations

- Stage 1's offline result is a statement about the simulator as much as about the bandit: a listener who scores taste with the page's own vectors favours the page. The live A/B on Tansa is the only judge of the two.
- The language-model methods saw 2,400 prompt visits (`sft`) and 400 (`grpo`, `dpo`) of LoRA on a 1.5B model, which is what about $40 of GPU time bought; a larger model could change every LLM row, and the study speaks only for this scale.
- The validation numbers for the language-model methods come from a 500-listener subset with intervals of about ± 0.015 on NDCG@10, which is why the debiasing sweep cannot separate its three weights.
- Candidates are filtered to the genre of the listener's most recent album, and one listener in three has no held-out album in that genre and so no prompt; the study speaks for listeners who stay in a genre.
- The base-model GRPO controls (`grpo` and `grpo_debiased`) were skipped on cost, so GRPO from the base model is known only from the 100-step pilot, and every GRPO row in the table starts from the SFT adapter.
- The opaque ablation is read under the strict format rule, so a fine-tuned seed that answered opaque prompts with bare indices scores zero there; we describe that in words rather than add a lenient index reading after seeing the data.

## Future directions

The first next step is live: the "Tune to me" mode is on a preview of Tansa, and real sessions with a 0.1 exploration floor would let the same SNIPS check run on real visitors instead of a simulated listener. For the study, the per-genre and per-popularity-decile breakdowns the protocol asks for could show whether the flat picture hides genres where the model does learn something, and the skipped base-model control would say whether the SFT start was the reason GRPO had nothing to amplify.

Beyond that, it would also be interesting to give the policy a reward it already partly earns. The learning check showed GRPO needs a base policy that is sometimes right for the right reason, and a reward model trained on listener preferences over sound (a small head on the audio embeddings, with a larger audio-text model as the gold reward) could be that. And since every method's recommendations already land on Tansa's map, a fun direction could be a Unimap of them, where each region is a method's recommendations sized by how many listeners it serves, so that the drift toward popular regions reads at a glance.
