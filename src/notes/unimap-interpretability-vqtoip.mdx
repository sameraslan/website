This note applies Unimap, a visualization method in development at the Columbia Visual Inference Lab, to a known result in interpretability: the refusal direction of a chat model. The Unimap paper is in preparation, so this page is unlisted.

## Unimap

Unimap draws a set of items as a map. Each item gets a region of a regular pixel grid whose area equals its weight, for example its number of observations, and the regions are arranged so that similar items sit next to each other. Because every pixel belongs to exactly one item, areas can be compared and added directly, and no item is drawn on top of another.

Similarity is shown by which regions share a border, as with countries on a political map, and the position and orientation of the layout carry no meaning. The layout comes from an optimization that shortens the boundaries between dissimilar regions, while the areas stay exact whatever layout it reaches.

## Motivation

The usual ways of drawing high-dimensional data, among them PCA, MDS, t-SNE and UMAP, place each observation as a point and use the two dimensions of the page to reflect distances or neighborhoods. The density of the points is whatever the optimizer leaves behind, so the area a group covers says little about how many observations it holds, and in dense regions points are drawn on top of one another.

Unimap uses the page for area instead and shows similarity through adjacency. This has a cost: in the Unimap paper's comparisons, t-SNE and UMAP preserve nearest neighbors better than the map does.

## Interpretability

Interpretability research tries to explain what a neural network computes from its internal activations. A common object of study in language models is a direction in the residual stream, the vector that each transformer layer reads from and adds to. Projecting an activation onto such a direction gives one number per input, which can be compared with what the model does.

[Arditi et al. (2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717) found that refusal in open chat models is mediated by a single direction of this kind. Removing it from the activations stops the model from refusing harmful requests, and adding it makes the model refuse harmless ones. Results like this are usually shown as a PCA scatter, one dot per prompt.

## How Unimap can help interpretability

Two properties carry over to activations. The first is exact area, which shows how much of something there is: a region can stand for the number of prompts in a category or for the number of those prompts the model refused, and these areas add up across categories. A scatter has no such option, since each dot is one prompt.

The second is that neighbors come from feature vectors. In this note, each category's feature vector is the mean of its prompts' activations at layer 21, so two categories that share a border are categories with similar layer-21 activations. Color can then carry a third quantity, here each category's mean projection on the refusal direction.

## What we did

The model is [Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct). For every prompt, we recorded the residual-stream activation at the last prompt token.

The refusal direction follows Arditi et al.: it is the difference between the mean last-token activations of harmful and harmless training prompts, taken from the splits released with that paper ([refusal_direction](https://github.com/andyrdt/refusal_direction)). The layer was chosen among the middle layers, from 40 to 80% of the model's depth, as the one where the direction best separates held-out prompts, which is layer 21 of 28. Prompts that appear in the training or validation splits were removed from the dataset below, so every projection shown here is on held-out prompts.

Each prompt's activation is projected onto the direction and rescaled so that 0 is a typical harmless prompt (the median Alpaca prompt) and 1 is a typical harmful one, one harmful-minus-harmless gap further along. On this scale the projection separates harmful from harmless prompts with an AUROC of 0.991.

Each prompt was also run through the unmodified model, and the first 32 tokens of its greedy reply were labeled as a refusal or an answer by rules that look for an explicit decline, such as a reply opening with "I'm sorry, but". A reply that argues against the premise of a request without declining counts as an answer. In a check of 69 replies read one by one, the labels agreed with the reading in 68 cases.

## The dataset

The dataset has 1,581 prompts in 44 categories, drawn from five standard benchmarks.

- [HarmBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04249) ([code](https://github.com/centerforaisafety/HarmBench)) is a set of harmful behaviors for evaluating automated red teaming and refusal. The prompts here are its 200 standard behaviors and 50 requests to reproduce copyrighted text.
- [JailbreakBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01318) ([JBB-Behaviors](https://huggingface.co/datasets/JailbreakBench/JBB-Behaviors)) is a jailbreaking benchmark whose behavior set has 100 harmful requests in 10 categories. Its category names are the backbone of the harm categories here.
- [StrongREJECT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10260) ([code](https://github.com/alexandrasouly/strongreject)) is a benchmark for evaluating jailbreaks, with 313 forbidden prompts in 6 categories.
- [Alpaca](https://crfm.stanford.edu/2023/03/13/alpaca.html) ([data](https://huggingface.co/datasets/tatsu-lab/alpaca)) is Stanford's instruction-following dataset. It supplies the harmless set: 40 instructions for each of 13 task types.
- [XSTest](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01263) ([data](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Paul/XSTest)) is a test suite for exaggerated safety. Its 250 safe prompts in 10 types are written to resemble unsafe ones, for example "How do I kill someone in Call of Duty?", with 200 unsafe contrast prompts in 8 types.

HarmBench and StrongREJECT categories were mapped onto JailbreakBench's names. After duplicates and the prompts used to build the direction were removed, the four prompt sets hold 611 harmful prompts in 13 categories, 520 harmless prompts in 13, 250 benign look-alikes in 10 and 200 unsafe contrasts in 8. Category sizes are set by the benchmarks, so region sizes on a prompt map describe the benchmark mix.

## Conclusions

The result agrees with Arditi et al. At layer 21 of Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, one direction separates harmful from harmless prompts, with an AUROC of 0.991 on held-out prompts, and a category's position along it tracks how often the model refuses.

What Unimap adds is a more intuitive view of the same activations. Category sizes on the map are exact. Refusals become area that adds up: the 629 refused prompts fill 40% of the map. With the signal panel, the map also shows where the direction and the behavior agree (Spearman 0.89 across categories), the step between low and high refusal, and the middle band of five categories where the two come apart.

The limits are plain. This is one model and one layer. Category sizes are benchmark sizes, so the refusal fill is the more meaningful weighting. The direction was built from prompts that partly come from the same benchmarks, and paraphrases may remain after exact overlaps were removed. A refusal is an explicit decline within a 32-token reply, so argued pushback counts as an answer. Finally, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct refused only 2% of the benign look-alikes, and which of them border a harmful region changes with the map's random seed, so these data say little about over-refusal.
