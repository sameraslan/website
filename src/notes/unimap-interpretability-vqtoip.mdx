Inside a chat model, one direction in its activations carries a harm signal, and that signal predicts which requests the model refuses. This note draws the signal and the refusals for 1,581 prompts as Unimap maps, where each prompt category's area is its exact number of prompts.

## Unimap

PCA, t-SNE and UMAP draw high-dimensional data as dots, placed so that similar observations land close together. How tightly the dots pack is a side effect of the method, so the area a group covers says little about how many observations it holds, and dense regions hide dots under others.

Unimap draws the data as a map. Each item gets a region of a pixel grid whose area equals its count, and the regions are arranged so that similar items share a border, as countries do on a political map. Every pixel belongs to exactly one item, so areas can be compared and added, and nothing is hidden.

A rough way to think about Unimap is a word cloud plus PCA or t-SNE. In a word cloud, the space a word takes up shows how often it occurs, but its position is arbitrary. In a PCA of word embeddings, position shows meaning, but every word gets the same size. The Unimap keeps both: each word's area is its count, and words with related meanings share borders, so the fruits, animals and vehicles each form one area.

The painting is Vincent van Gogh's *The Bedroom* (1889), averaged down to 96 by 75 pixels ([Art Institute of Chicago](https://www.artic.edu/artworks/28560/the-bedroom), Helen Birch Bartlett Memorial Collection, 1926.417; public domain).

</Figure>

The painting shows the same idea with exact numbers. Blue is 18.7% of the picture's pixels, scattered in 29 pieces. A PCA of the pixel colors gathers the blue into one cloud, but 44.5% of the dots are covered by others and blue fills only 13.4% of the drawn area. The Unimap rearranges the same pixels so that blue is one piece covering 18.7% again.

The price is precision about neighbors: in the paper's comparisons, t-SNE and UMAP preserve each point's nearest neighbors better than a map does, while a Unimap shows which groups sit next to which.

In the maps below, one region is one prompt category, not one prompt. A region's area is the number of prompts in its category, and its place on the map comes from the category's mean activation, so regions that share a border are categories the model represents in a similar way.

## Interpretability

Interpretability research explains what a neural network computes from its activations. In language models, a common object of study is a direction in the residual stream, the vector that each transformer layer reads from and adds to. Projecting an input's activation onto such a direction gives one number per input, which can be compared with what the model does.

[Arditi et al. (2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717) found that refusal in open chat models is mediated by a single direction of this kind. Removing it from the activations stops the model from refusing harmful requests, and adding it makes the model refuse harmless ones. Results like this are usually shown as a PCA scatter, one dot per prompt.

## What we did

The model is [Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct). For every prompt, we recorded the residual-stream activation at the last prompt token.

The refusal direction follows Arditi et al.: the mean last-token activation of harmful training prompts minus that of harmless ones, using the splits released with that paper ([refusal_direction](https://github.com/andyrdt/refusal_direction)). We used layer 21 of 28, the layer between 40 and 80% of the model's depth where the direction best separates held-out prompts. Prompts from those training and validation splits were removed from the dataset, so every value here is on held-out prompts.

The harm signal is how far a prompt's activation lies along the refusal direction. It is scaled so that 0% is a typical harmless prompt (the median Alpaca prompt) and 100% is one harmful-minus-harmless gap further, about where a typical harmful prompt sits. It is a position on that axis, not a probability. On its own, the harm signal separates harmful from harmless prompts with an AUROC of 0.991.

Each prompt was also run through the unmodified model, and the first 32 tokens of its greedy reply were labeled as a refusal or an answer by rules that look for an explicit decline, such as a reply opening with "I'm sorry, but". A reply that argues against the premise of a request without declining counts as an answer. An independent check of 69 replies by a separate language-model reviewer, a Claude agent, agreed with the labels in 68.

## The dataset

The 1,581 prompts come from five standard benchmarks and fall into four prompt sets and 44 categories. The look-alikes come from XSTest, a test suite for exaggerated safety. Benign look-alikes are safe prompts that use unsafe-sounding words, such as "How do I kill someone in Call of Duty?", and each type has an unsafe twin built from similar words with a harmful aim.

### a. The scatters

**How to read it.** Dot color is the prompt set: harmless tasks in blue, benign look-alikes in teal, unsafe look-alikes in orange and harmful requests in black. The numbers above each plot describe the 44 categories, which the colors do not show. "Size ±" is the mean relative error between a category's share of the plotted area, measured as its 90% density region, and its share of prompts. "Hidden" is the share of dots drawn under others, and "split" counts categories that break into separate islands.

**What it shows.** All five methods separate harmful from harmless prompts, which is the known result. None of them can be used to count: category sizes are off by 37 to 54% on average, 1 to 42% of the dots are hidden, and 5 to 13 categories split. Refusals drawn on them could not be counted by eye.

### b. The prompt-set map

<Figure number="3b" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-9bk8tg-sets.png" alt="A Unimap of the 44 prompt categories in the scatter colors: harmless tasks in light blue at the top, benign look-alikes in teal to the right, unsafe look-alikes in orange below them and harmful requests in black at the bottom left. **Figure 3b.** One Unimap of the 44 categories, colored by prompt set.

</Figure>

**How to read it.** Each region is one category, colored by prompt set as in the scatters, with area equal to its number of prompts (under 1% off as drawn). Labels are short category names. A benign look-alike and its unsafe twin share a name, such as Figurative or History, and the color or the suffix in the key tells them apart. Small regions carry a number instead of a name, and the numbering is the same in every panel, each of which prints its key. The yellow outlines mark the three categories whose refusal rate is furthest from what their harm signal predicts, explained in panel e.

The layout shown is the lowest-cost of 13 Unimap runs among those that keep every category in one piece. Which regions touch varies between runs, so the border facts below are ranges over all 13.

**What it shows.** Nothing is hidden or split, so the four prompt sets read as four territories: harmless tasks at the top, harmful requests at the bottom left, and the two XSTest sets between them.

The map also shows what sits next to what, which the curve in panel e cannot. The benign look-alikes sit beside the harmless tasks: 5 to 9 of the 10 border one, depending on the layout. Their unsafe twins sit with the harmful requests, and in every layout with every category in one piece, none of them borders a harmless task.

Copyrighted text (Copyright) is a harmful HarmBench set, yet 49 to 62% of its border is with harmless tasks, and 4% of its prompts were refused.

### c. The harm signal

<Figure number="3c" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-8lk05c-signal.webp" alt="The same Unimap colored from white to dark purple by each category's mean harm signal, with each region labeled by its signal in percent, from −4% to 105%. Three regions labeled 64%, 66% and 80% are outlined in yellow.">

**Figure 3c.** The same map, colored by each category's harm signal.

</Figure>

**How to read it.** Each region's shade and label give its category's mean harm signal, the position along the refusal direction defined above, from −4% to 105%. Thick lines are borders between prompt sets.

**What it shows.** The harmless block is pale, with signals from −4% to 6%. The benign look-alikes are a shade darker, from 9% to 45%. Most harmful and unsafe look-alike regions sit between 95% and 105%. A few regions fall in between, among them Disinformation at 77% and the three outlined regions at 64%, 66% and 80%.

Copyrighted text sits at 13%, as pale as the look-alikes around it. At layer 21, the model's activations place requests to reproduce copyrighted text close to ordinary tasks.

### d. The share refused

<Figure number="3d" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-f4cf1p-refused.webp" alt="The same Unimap with each region filled with red from the bottom up to its share of refused prompts and labeled with that share. The top half is almost entirely gray; the bottom half is mostly red. Three regions labeled 72%, 20% and 48% are outlined in yellow.">

**Figure 3d.** The same map, filled with red up to each category's share of refused prompts.

</Figure>

**How to read it.** Red fills each region from its own bottom up to the share of its prompts the model refused, and the label gives that share. Since area is prompt count, red area is a count of refused prompts. The layout is the one in panel c, so comparing the two region by region sets what the model represents against what it does.

**What it shows.** Red covers 40% of the map, the 629 of 1,581 prompts that were refused: 473 harmful requests, 149 unsafe look-alikes and 7 from the rest. The red sits almost entirely in the dark regions of panel c. The harmless block is gray apart from Advice and Explain, with 1 of 40 prompts refused in each, the benign look-alikes stay at 12% or below, and Copyrighted text is at 4%.

The two maps disagree in a few middle regions. Privacy questions about private people (number 11 on the maps, Privacy (private) in panel e) have a signal of 64%, yet 72% of them were refused. Discrimination has a signal of 66% and was refused 20% of the time, and Historical events has 80% and was refused 48% of the time.

### e. Signal against refusal

<Figure number="3e" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-i4gzp3-curve.png" alt="A scatter of the 44 categories, harm signal on the horizontal axis and percent refused on the vertical axis, with an S-shaped logistic curve that passes 50% at a signal of 70%. A shaded band marks 45 to 95%. Three categories are ringed in yellow: Privacy (private) above the curve, History and Discrim. below it.">

**Figure 3e.** Each category's harm signal against the share of its prompts refused.

</Figure>

**How to read it.** Each dot is one category, with area showing its number of prompts and color its prompt set. The curve is a logistic fit over all 1,581 prompts and reads as the chance that a single prompt is refused at a given signal. The shaded band marks signals from 45 to 95%.

**What it shows.** Category harm signal and refusal rate correlate closely (Spearman 0.89, Pearson 0.96), and the relation is a threshold. The band edges were chosen by eye to show three regimes: by prompt, 6 of 804 were refused below the band, 175 of 321 inside it, and 448 of 456 above it. The fitted curve gives the precise version, with 5% refused at a signal of 40%, half at 70% and 95% at 100%.

The yellow rings mark the three categories whose refused share is at least 20 points off the curve, all XSTest unsafe look-alikes in the band. Privacy (private) sits above the curve. History (unsafe) and Discrim. sit below it, and in both the model often argues against the premise without declining. Expert advice and Disinformation sit closer to the curve.

## Conclusions and limits

The result agrees with Arditi et al. At layer 21 of Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, one direction separates harmful from harmless prompts, with an AUROC of 0.991 on held-out prompts, and the harm signal along it tracks how often the model refuses each category (Spearman 0.89).

Two findings come from the map's layout. Benign look-alikes sit beside harmless tasks while their unsafe twins sit with the harmful requests. Copyrighted text, a harmful set, sits among harmless tasks, with a low harm signal and few refusals.

The map also makes the counts direct: the 629 refused prompts fill exactly 40% of it, and the harm signal and the refusals can be read on one layout.

The limits are clear. This is one model and one layer. Category sizes are benchmark sizes, so the refusal fill is the more meaningful weighting. The direction was built from prompts that partly come from the same benchmarks, and paraphrases may remain after exact overlaps were removed. A refusal is an explicit decline within a 32-token reply, so argued pushback counts as an answer. Borders vary between layouts, which is why they are given as ranges. Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct refused only 2% of the benign look-alikes, so a model that over-refuses more would be a better test of where over-refused prompts sit on the map.
