Unimap is a new visualization algorithm for high-dimensional data of any kind, from brain recordings to the internals of a neural network. This note uses it to look inside the chat model Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct at a question for both AI safety and interpretability: what in its activations decides whether it refuses a request. One direction carries a harm signal that predicts refusals, and the maps show with exact counts which kinds of prompts carry it.

## Unimap

PCA, t-SNE and UMAP draw high-dimensional data as dots, with similar items close together, but the area a group covers says little about how many items it holds. Unimap draws the data as a map: each item gets a region whose area equals its count, and similar items share a border, as countries do on a political map.

A rough way to think about Unimap is a word cloud plus PCA or t-SNE, and the two rows under each panel mark what each view gets right. The Unimap keeps both: each word's area is its count, and words with related meanings share borders, so the fruits, animals and vehicles each form one area. A painting shows the same idea with real data.

**Figure 2. The same pixels shown three ways.** The black outline marks the blue pixels in each panel. The PCA's dots pile up and hide each other, so its counts are hard to read. The price is precision about neighbors: in comparisons for the Unimap paper (in preparation), t-SNE and UMAP preserve each point's nearest neighbors better than a map does. With that intuition, the next step is to apply Unimap to a model's internals.

## Interpretability

Interpretability research explains what a neural network computes from its internal activations. [Arditi et al. (2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717) found that refusal in open chat models is mediated by a single direction in these activations, the refusal direction: removing it stops the model from refusing harmful requests, and adding it makes the model refuse harmless ones.

Results like this are usually shown as a PCA scatter, one dot per prompt, which leaves two questions open: how common each prompt category is, and how strongly each pushes along the refusal direction. A Unimap with one region per category answers both.

## How the maps were made

<Figure number="3" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-0u9ovu-activation.webp" alt="A five-step diagram. 1: the prompt Write a limerick poem, one of 40 prompts in Poems and stories. 2: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct reads its tokens through 28 layers, and the activation is read at layer 21 at the last token. 3: the activation, a vector of 3,584 numbers. 4: the Poems and stories vector, the average over its 40 prompts. 5: 44 category vectors go into Unimap, one region per category. A side box takes the dot product of the prompt's vector with the refusal direction, giving a harm signal of −0.4%.">

**Figure 3. From a prompt to a map.** One prompt, "Write a limerick poem", goes into the map in five steps, and the side box computes its harm signal.

</Figure>

An activation is the vector of numbers a model holds for each token at each layer, 3,584 numbers in Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. It is read at layer 21 of 28 at the last token, because there the model has read the whole prompt and is about to answer. A category's vector is the average over its prompts, so it stands for what those prompts have in common. On the maps, one region is one prompt category, not one prompt: its area is its number of prompts, and bordering regions are categories with similar vectors.

The refusal direction follows Arditi et al.: the average activation of harmful training prompts minus that of harmless ones, from the splits released with that paper ([refusal_direction](https://github.com/andyrdt/refusal_direction)). Layer 21 is the layer between 40 and 80% depth where this direction best separates held-out prompts, and prompts from those splits were removed.

The harm signal is how far a prompt's activation lies along the refusal direction, scaled so that 0% is a typical harmless prompt (the median Alpaca prompt) and 100% is one harmful-minus-harmless gap further. It is a position, not a probability, and on its own it separates harmful from harmless prompts with an AUROC of 0.991.

Refusal labels come from rules that look for an explicit decline, such as "I'm sorry, but", in the first 32 tokens of the unmodified model's greedy reply; a reply that argues against the premise without declining counts as an answer. An independent check of 69 replies by a separate language-model reviewer, a Claude agent, agreed with the labels in 68.

## The dataset

The 1,581 prompts in 44 categories come from five standard benchmarks. Benign look-alikes, from the XSTest suite for exaggerated safety, are safe prompts with unsafe-sounding words, such as "How do I kill someone in Call of Duty?", and each type has an unsafe twin with similar words and a harmful aim. The figures abbreviate JailbreakBench as JBB.

## What the maps show

### a. The scatters

<Figure number="4a" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-v7ha1w-scatters.webp" alt="Five scatter plots of 1,581 prompts laid out by PCA, t-SNE, UMAP, MDS and Isomap, dots colored by prompt set, each titled with its size error, hidden-dot share and split count.">

**Figure 4a.** The layer-21 activations laid out by PCA, t-SNE, UMAP, MDS and Isomap, one dot per prompt.

</Figure>

**How to read it.** Dot color is the prompt set. "Size ±" is the mean relative error between a category's share of the plotted area (its 90% density region) and its share of prompts, "hidden" is the share of dots drawn under others, and "split" counts categories broken into separate islands.

**What it shows.** All five methods separate harmful from harmless prompts, but none can be used to count: category sizes are off by 37 to 54% on average, 1 to 42% of the dots are hidden, and 5 to 13 categories split.

### b. The prompt-set map

<Figure number="4b" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-9bk8tg-sets.png" alt="A Unimap of the 44 prompt categories in the scatter colors: harmless tasks in light blue at the top, benign look-alikes in teal to the right, unsafe look-alikes in orange below them and harmful requests in black at the bottom left. Three regions are outlined in yellow.">

**Figure 4b.** One Unimap of the 44 categories, colored by prompt set. The layout is the lowest-cost of 13 runs that keep every category in one piece.

</Figure>

**How to read it.** Each region is one category, with area equal to its number of prompts (under 1% off as drawn). A benign look-alike and its unsafe twin share a short name, such as Figurative or History, told apart by color or by the suffix in the key. Small regions carry a number, listed under each panel; a number means the same category in every panel. Yellow outlines mark the three categories whose refusal rate is furthest from what their harm signal predicts (panel e).

**What it shows.** The four prompt sets form four territories, and the map shows what sits next to what, which panel e cannot. Over the 13 layouts, 5 to 9 of the 10 benign look-alikes border a harmless task, while their unsafe twins sit with the harmful requests and, in every layout that keeps each category in one piece, none borders a harmless task. Copyrighted text (Copyright), a harmful HarmBench set, shares 49 to 62% of its border with harmless tasks, and 4% of its prompts were refused.

### c. The harm signal

<Figure number="4c" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-8lk05c-signal.webp" alt="The same Unimap colored from white to dark purple by each category's mean harm signal, with each region labeled by its signal in percent, from −4% to 105%. Three regions labeled 64%, 66% and 80% are outlined in yellow.">

**Figure 4c.** The same map, with shade and label giving each category's average harm signal, with thick lines between prompt sets.

</Figure>

**What it shows.** Harmless tasks are pale, from −4% to 6%, and benign look-alikes a shade darker, from 9% to 45%. Most harmful and unsafe look-alike regions sit between 95% and 105%, with a few in between, among them Disinformation at 77% and the outlined regions at 64%, 66% and 80%. Copyrighted text sits at 13%, as pale as its harmless neighbors.

### d. The share refused

<Figure number="4d" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-f4cf1p-refused.webp" alt="The same Unimap with each region filled with red from the bottom up to its share of refused prompts and labeled with that share. The top half is almost entirely gray; the bottom half is mostly red. Three regions labeled 72%, 20% and 48% are outlined in yellow.">

**Figure 4d.** The same map, with red filling each region from its bottom up to the share of its prompts the model refused, so red area counts refused prompts.

</Figure>

**What it shows.** Red covers 40% of the map, the 629 of 1,581 refused prompts: 473 harmful requests, 149 unsafe look-alikes and 7 from the rest, among them 1 of 40 prompts each in Advice and Explain. Compared region by region with panel c, the maps disagree in the outlined regions: privacy questions about private people (Privacy (private) in panel b, number 11 in panels c and d) have a signal of 64% and 72% refused, while Discrim. at 66% and History (unsafe) at 80% were refused 20% and 48% of the time.

### e. Signal against refusal

<Figure number="4e" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-i4gzp3-curve.png" alt="A scatter of the 44 categories, harm signal on the horizontal axis and percent refused on the vertical axis, with an S-shaped logistic curve that passes 50% at a signal of 70%. A shaded band marks 45 to 95%. Three categories are ringed in yellow: Privacy (private) above the curve, History and Discrim. below it.">

**Figure 4e.** Each category's harm signal against the share of its prompts refused.

</Figure>

**How to read it.** Each dot is one category, sized by prompts and colored by set, and the curve, a logistic fit over all 1,581 prompts, gives the chance that one prompt is refused at a given signal. The band edges at 45 and 95% were chosen by eye to show three regimes.

**What it shows.** Signal and refusal rate correlate closely (Spearman 0.89, Pearson 0.96), and the relation is a threshold: 6 of 804 prompts were refused below the band, 175 of 321 inside it and 448 of 456 above it, and the curve gives 5% refused at a signal of 40%, half at 70% and 95% at 100%. The three ringed categories, all XSTest unsafe look-alikes, sit at least 20 points off the curve: Privacy (private) above it, and History (unsafe) and Discrim. below it, where the model often argues against the premise without declining.

## Across layers and models

Representational similarity analysis (RSA) checks how general the maps' layout is by comparing two tables of distances between the 44 categories: one measured in a model's activations, the geometry the map draws, and one built from a candidate explanation. One explanation is the prompts' wording, from distances between text embeddings; the other is the refusal direction. The score is the correlation between the tables.

<Figure number="5" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-tp99b1-rsa.png" alt="Left: a line chart of how well wording and the refusal direction match the category distances in five model families, from 25% to 100% depth. Wording leads early, the refusal direction from mid-depth, with a shaded crossover zone and a gray band for the best score possible given noise. Right: for each family, wording fit minus refusal fit moves from the refusal side to the wording side after the refusal direction is removed.">

**Figure 5. What sorts the prompt categories inside five models.** Left: how well each explanation matches the category distances, from 25% depth to the last layer, for single model families (thin) and their average (bold); the gray band is the best score possible given sampling noise. Right: wording fit minus refusal fit at each family's refusal layer, before (open) and after (filled) removing the refusal direction.

</Figure>

The five families are Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen3-8B, OLMo-2-7B, Granite-3.1-8B and Falcon3-7B. At 25% depth wording matches better in every family; the refusal direction takes the lead between 39 and 75% depth, depending on the family, and keeps it. Removing that single direction, one of 3,584 to 4,096, hands the layout back to wording in all five. The families also arrange the categories alike, with correlations of 0.80 to 0.93 between their distance tables.

The refusal direction is one very loud direction, so it dominates the raw distances the map draws. Measured against how much prompts vary within a category, it is one direction among many wording directions, and wording leads at every layer.

## Conclusions

One direction in a 3,584-number activation predicts, category by category, how often Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct refuses, and the relation is a threshold: half of prompts are refused at a harm signal of 70% (Spearman 0.89 across 44 categories).

The map adds three things. It counts where the refusals come from: 629 refused prompts fill 40% of it, 473 of them harmful requests and 149 unsafe look-alikes. It shows where a benchmark and the model disagree: HarmBench labels copyrighted text harmful, but the model gives it a harm signal of 13%, refuses 4% of it and places it beside harmless tasks. And it shows where signal and behavior part ways: the model mostly argues with the History (unsafe) and Discrim. prompts instead of refusing them.

Across five model families, the layout follows wording early and the refusal direction from mid-depth, though wording leads at every layer when measured against the spread within each category.

The core result agrees with Arditi et al., who showed that a single direction mediates refusal in open chat models.

## Limits

- The maps and refusal labels cover one model at one layer; the five-family comparison covers only the layout.
- Category sizes come from the benchmarks, so region sizes describe the benchmark mix, not real-world traffic.
- The direction was built partly from the same benchmarks; exact overlaps were removed, but paraphrases may remain and make the separation look cleaner.
- A refusal is an explicit decline within a 32-token reply, so a reply that argues against the request counts as an answer.
- The model refused only 2% of the benign look-alikes, so these data say little about over-refusal; a model that over-refuses more would test it better.
