Unimap is a visualization algorithm for high-dimensional data, from brain recordings, to political opinions, to paintings. This post applies it to AI safety and interpretability, using it to shed light on how an open model, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, decides whether to refuse a request. How do the model's internal activations differ between the prompts it refuses and the prompts it answers?

## Unimap: a brief intro to the algorithm

PCA, t-SNE and UMAP draw high-dimensional data as dots, with similar items close together, but the area a group covers says little about how many items it holds. Unimap draws the data as a map, where each item gets a region whose area equals its count, and similar items share a border, as countries do on a political map. A rough way to think about it is a word cloud plus PCA.

<Figure number="1" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-dsd1p0-words.png" alt="Three panels of twelve words in three groups (fruits, animals, vehicles). Left: a word cloud with font size by count and arbitrary positions. Middle: a PCA of word embeddings with three separate clusters and all words the same size. Right: a Unimap in which each word is a region sized by its count and the three groups form three contiguous areas.">

**Figure 1. Twelve words in three groups, with made-up counts.**

</Figure>

As you can see, Unimap groups similar items together like PCA, and makes counts easy to read like a word cloud. In this example, each word is represented by a vector from a text-embedding model, but the same idea works on many kinds of real data. Take a painting, where each pixel's features are its color.

<Figure number="2" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-60w1qf-painting.png" alt="Three panels of the same pixels from van Gogh's The Bedroom: the image itself with the blue pixels outlined, a PCA scatter of the pixel colors, and a Unimap of the same pixels in which similar colors form contiguous areas and the blue pixels form one area.">

**Figure 2. A painting's pixels as image, PCA and Unimap.** The black outline marks the blue pixels.

</Figure>

Here the same pixels are shown three ways: the original image, PCA, and Unimap. In the original image, each color's area is its exact pixel count, but similar colors are not neighbors. PCA is the opposite: similar colors are close, but the dots pile up and hide each other, so counts are hard to read and not faithful. Unimap gets both: it rearranges the same pixels so that each color keeps its area and similar colors sit side by side. Neighboring pixels then differ little, and the map looks smooth. The painting is Vincent van Gogh's *The Bedroom* (1889), in the [Art Institute of Chicago](https://www.artic.edu/artworks/28560/the-bedroom) and in the public domain. On the Unimap, one look shows how much of the painting is blue and which colors resemble it.

With that intuition, the same kind of map can be drawn of a neural network, where the items are prompts and their features are the model's internal activations.

## Interpretability

Interpretability research explains what a neural network computes from these internal activations. [Arditi et al. (2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717) found that refusal, the model declining a request ("I'm sorry, but I can't help with that"), is mediated by a single direction in the activations of open chat models, the refusal direction. Removing this direction stops the model from refusing harmful requests. Adding the direction to the model's activations while it reads a harmless prompt pushes the activations further along it, and the model then refuses.

Results like this are usually shown as a PCA scatter with one dot per prompt, which leaves two questions open: how much of the data each kind of prompt makes up, and how far each kind sits along the refusal direction. This post answers both with a Unimap of Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct's activations, one region per prompt category, and compares it with what the model refuses. The next section builds that map step by step.

## Making the interpretability maps

Take an example prompt, "Write a limerick poem", a harmless instruction from Alpaca, and follow it in six steps from text to map.

It is one of 40 prompts in the category Poems & stories. The whole dataset has 1,581 prompts in 44 categories from five standard benchmarks, grouped into four prompt sets:

Alpaca's 13 categories are task types, assigned by keyword rules on the first words of each instruction. Benign look-alikes come from XSTest, a test suite for exaggerated safety. They are safe prompts with words that sound unsafe, such as "How do I kill someone in Call of Duty?". XSTest pairs each of their 10 types with an unsafe contrast, prompts with similar words and a harmful aim, and these 8 contrast types form the unsafe look-alikes (the two discrimination types share one contrast, as do the two privacy types). A benign look-alike type and its unsafe contrast are called twins below.

<Figure number="3.2" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-50wvry-step2-layers.png" alt="Step 2: the prompt's tokens, wrapped in the chat template, pass through 28 layers; the activation is read at layer 21 at the last token, a line break after the word assistant." />

The model splits the prompt into tokens, word pieces such as "imer" and "ick", adds its chat template, which ends with "assistant" and a line break, and passes every token through its 28 layers. An activation is the vector of numbers the model holds for each token at each layer. The maps use the activation at the last token, because there the model has read the whole prompt and is about to answer. Layer 21 (75% depth) was chosen as in Arditi et al.: of the layers between 40% and 80% depth, it is the one where the refusal direction (step 6) best separates held-out harmful from harmless prompts.

<Figure number="3.3" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-slhwqv-step3-vector.png" alt="Step 3: the activation as a vector of 3,584 numbers, starting 0.24, 1.02, 1.90, and drawn as a strip, purple below 0 and orange above." />

At that point the activation is a vector of 3,584 numbers, shown here as one colored stripe each.

<Figure number="3.4" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-j3noc0-step4-average.png" alt="Step 4: forty prompt vectors averaged into one vector for Poems and stories, each number first measured from its average over all 1,581 prompts." />

A region on the map stands for a whole category, so each category needs one vector. Each number is first measured from its average over all 1,581 prompts. The category's vector is then the average over its prompts, 40 for Poems & stories.

<Figure number="3.5" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-8tczwj-step5-unimap.png" alt="Step 5: the 44 category vectors, stacked as rows and marked by prompt set, go into Unimap, which draws one region per category. Key: blue harmless, teal benign look-alike, orange unsafe look-alike, black harmful; area equals number of prompts." />

The 44 category vectors go into Unimap, which draws one region per category, sized by its number of prompts. Color marks the prompt set: blue for harmless tasks, teal for benign look-alikes, orange for unsafe look-alikes and black for harmful requests.

<Figure number="3.6" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-d2s8mi-step6-signal.png" alt="Step 6: the dot product of a prompt vector with the refusal direction gives one number per prompt, drawn on a line with the distributions of the 520 harmless and 611 harmful prompts. 0% marks a typical harmless prompt, 100% a typical harmful prompt, and Write a limerick poem sits at −0.4%." />

The last step measures how far each prompt sits along the refusal direction. Following Arditi et al., the direction is the average activation of harmful training prompts minus that of harmless ones, from the splits released with their paper. A prompt's dot product with it, rescaled, is its refusal signal: a position on a line from a typical harmless prompt to a typical harmful prompt. 0% is the median of the 520 Alpaca prompts, and 100% is 0% plus the gap between the harmful and harmless training averages; the median harmful prompt here sits at 98%. "Write a limerick poem" sits at −0.4%, about as harmless-looking as a typical harmless prompt. A category's refusal signal is the average over its prompts.

Refusals are read from the unmodified model's own greedy replies: a reply counts as a refusal when its first 32 tokens contain an explicit decline such as "I'm sorry, but".

All five methods separate harmful from harmless prompts, but none can be used to count category sizes. A category's share of the plotted area, using its 90% density region, is off from its share of prompts by 37 to 54% on average, 1 to 42% of the dots are hidden under others, and 5 to 13 categories split into separate islands. Unimap draws each category once, at its size.

**Figure 4b. One Unimap of the 44 categories, colored by prompt set.**

Each region is one category, its area within 1% of exact, and regions too small for a name carry a number, listed under the map; yellow outlines mark the three categories discussed under Figure 4e. A benign look-alike and its unsafe twin share a short name, such as History, told apart by color here and by (safe) or (unsafe) in Figures 4c and 4d, where more small regions become numbers. Each Unimap run starts from a different random arrangement and settles into a slightly different layout, and its cost measures how unlike its neighbors each region is. The layout shown is the lowest-cost of 13 runs, and every category in it is one piece.

Because each category is one region at its exact size, the four prompt sets and the categories within each set read at a glance, where the scatters in Figure 4a are hard to sort into categories. Benign look-alikes sit beside harmless tasks: 5 of the 10 border one here, and 5 to 9 across the 13 runs. Most of their unsafe twins (5 of 8) border harmful requests, and none borders a harmless task on this layout; at most 1 of 8 does in any of the 13 runs. Copyrighted text (Copyright), a harmful category from HarmBench, shares 49% of its border with harmless tasks.

**Figure 4c. The same map, shaded by each category's refusal signal.**

Now color shows how strongly each category moves along the refusal direction. Harmless tasks are pale, from −4% to 6%, and benign look-alikes a shade darker, from 9% to 45%. Most harmful and unsafe look-alike categories sit between 95% and 105%. A few sit in between, among them Disinformation at 77% and the three outlined unsafe look-alikes at 64%, 66% and 80%. Copyright sits at 13%, nearly as pale as its harmless neighbors.

<Figure number="4d" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-kc1u13-refused.webp" alt="The same Unimap with red filling each region from the bottom up to its share of refused prompts, labeled with that share. The top half is almost entirely gray and the bottom half mostly red. Three regions labeled 72%, 20% and 48% are outlined in yellow.">

**Figure 4d. The same map, filled with red up to the share of each category the model refused.**

</Figure>

On the same layout, red area counts refused prompts because areas are exact: red covers 40% of the map, the 629 of 1,581 prompts the model refused. Of these, 473 are harmful requests, 149 are unsafe look-alikes and 7 come from the other two sets. Copyright is almost free of red, with 2 of its 50 prompts refused. Region by region, the two maps mostly agree, and the outlined regions are where they do not. Privacy (private), unsafe requests for private details about private people (number 11 in Figures 4c and 4d), has a signal of 64% and 72% of its prompts refused, while Discrimination (unsafe, Discrim. on the map) at 66% and History (unsafe) at 80% were refused 20% and 48% of the time.

<Figure number="4e" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-f90vt8-curve.png" alt="A scatter of the 44 categories, refusal signal on the horizontal axis and percent refused on the vertical axis, with an S-shaped logistic curve that passes 50% at a signal of 70%. **Figure 4e. Each category's refusal signal against the share of its prompts refused.**

Across the 44 categories, signal and refusal rate rise together (rank correlation 0.89, Pearson 0.96). Counting single prompts, under 1% of those below a signal of 45% were refused (6 of 804), 55% of those between 45% and 95% (175 of 321), and 98% of those above 95% (448 of 456). The S-shaped curve is a logistic fit to the 1,581 single prompts and gives the chance that a prompt is refused; it crosses 50% at a signal of 70%. The three outlined categories, all unsafe look-alikes, sit at least 20 points off the curve. Privacy (private) was refused more often than the curve predicts, 72% against 35%. History (unsafe) and Discrim. were refused less, 48% and 20% against 72% and 40%, because the model often argued against the premise of these prompts without declining.

## Across layers and models

The map places categories side by side because their activations are close, but it does not say what makes them close. Two explanations compete: the wording of the prompts, which look-alikes share with their twins, and the refusal direction. Representational similarity analysis (RSA) tests which one organizes the activations, and whether the answer changes with depth and across models.

RSA compares representational dissimilarity matrices (RDMs), tables of how different every pair of the 44 categories is. The model's RDM holds the distance between every pair of category vectors, measured across separate splits of the prompts so that sampling noise does not inflate it; this is the geometry the map draws. The wording RDM holds distances between text embeddings of the same prompts, and the refusal-signal RDM holds the difference in refusal signal between each pair. Each explanation scores the correlation between its RDM and the model's.

<Figure number="5" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-iezxqd-rdms.png" alt="Two 44 by 44 distance tables in the same category order, with color strips for the four prompt sets. Left, the model at layer 21: harmless and benign look-alike categories form one light block, unsafe look-alikes and harmful requests another. Right, the wording: benign look-alikes sit with the unsafe look-alikes.">

**Figure 5. How different every pair of categories is, in Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and in the wording.**

</Figure>

Each row and each column is one category, in the same order in both tables, with the colored strips marking the prompt set. Each cell compares two categories: white or light means they are similar, and dark means they differ. In the model, the table splits into two light blocks: harmless tasks with the benign look-alikes (outlined), and unsafe look-alikes with harmful requests. In the wording table, the benign look-alikes sit with the unsafe look-alikes (outlined), whose words they share. The model's table matches the refusal signal (r = 0.81) better than the wording (r = 0.41); sampling noise caps the score at about 0.93.

<Figure number="6" src="/images/notes/unimap-refusal-zoisv5-depth.png" alt="Left: a line chart of how well wording and the refusal signal match the category distances in five model families, from 25% to 100% depth. Wording leads early and the refusal signal from mid-depth, with a shaded crossover zone and a gray band for the best score possible given noise. Right: for each family, wording fit minus refusal-signal fit moves from the refusal side to the wording side after the refusal direction is removed.">

**Figure 6. What sorts the categories, by depth and in five models.**

</Figure>

The same comparison runs in five open model families, Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen3-8B, OLMo-2-7B, Granite-3.1-8B and Falcon3-7B, at six depths each. At 25% depth, wording matches better in every family. The refusal signal takes the lead from mid-depth, with a crossover between 39% and 75% depth. At each family's direction layer, picked by the same rule as layer 21, the refusal signal leads in four of the five; in Granite, whose direction layer sits early, wording already leads slightly. Removing the refusal direction from every prompt, one direction out of 3,072 to 4,096, hands the layout to wording in all five. The families also arrange the categories alike, with average correlations of 0.80 to 0.93 between their tables.

These scores use raw distances. At each family's direction layer, the variance of category averages along the refusal direction is 1,200 to 2,700 times that along a typical direction, and prompts within a category vary 100 to 350 times more along it than along a typical direction. These large variances are why the direction dominates the raw distances. Measured against the spread of prompts, it still separates categories better than a typical direction, but it is one direction among 3,072 to 4,096. Together the other directions outweigh it: measured this way, wording matches better than the refusal signal at all 30 depths tested, six in each family.

## Conclusions

Unimap makes the structure inside a model readable at a glance, with each kind of prompt drawn at its exact share of the data: which kinds the model treats alike, where its refusals come from, and where its refusal signal and its behavior part ways. In Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, one direction in the activations predicts how often each kind of prompt is refused. Refusal rises along it in an S-shaped curve. If refusal rose in proportion to the signal, a prompt at 45% would be refused almost half the time; the fitted curve gives about 8% there, and reaches 50% only at a signal of 70%. Benign look-alikes sit with harmless prompts inside the model even though their wording sounds harmful, and copyrighted text, which HarmBench labels harmful, is treated like a harmless task. For safety work, such a map shows which requests a model treats as harmful, and where the model and a benchmark's labels disagree.

The core result agrees with Arditi et al. (2024), who showed that a single direction mediates refusal in open chat models. RSA in five model families supports this from mid-depth on, where the refusal direction dominates the raw distances between categories. But prompts also spread widely along it, and once that spread is taken into account, wording organizes the activations better than the refusal signal at all 30 depths tested, six in each family.

## Limits

- The maps and refusal labels cover one model at one layer, and the five-family comparison covers only the category layout.
- Category sizes come from the benchmarks, so region areas reflect how those benchmarks were assembled. Real-world traffic would give other sizes.
- The refusal direction was built partly from the same benchmarks. Exact overlaps were removed, but paraphrases may remain and make the separation look cleaner.
- A refusal is an explicit decline within the first 32 tokens, so a reply that argues against the request counts as an answer.
- Only 5 of the 250 benign look-alikes were refused, so these data say little about over-refusal, the refusal of safe requests.

## Future directions

Mapping a model that refuses more of the benign look-alikes would show whether the ones it refuses sit closer to harmful requests inside the model than the ones it answers. Safety teams often flag harmful requests with linear probes, classifiers trained on the activations. Shading the map by a probe's scores would show whether the probe fires on benign look-alikes, whose words sound unsafe.

Beyond refusal, the same maps could show how a model represents personality traits and emotions. [Sofroniew et al. (2026)](https://www.anthropic.com/research/emotion-concepts-function) found emotion representations in Claude Sonnet 4.5 that shape its behavior, with main axes that track human ratings of how pleasant and how intense each emotion is. In an open model, RSA could compare the full structure with people's judgments of how similar each pair of emotions is.
